# Part B1 — Extended synopsis (5 pages)

This is the document that decides Step 1. The panel reads it and the CV, and nothing else.
Write it for a reader who is a good scientist, is not in your subfield, and is reading their
eleventh proposal of the weekend.

## Suggested allocation

| Section | Pages |
|---|---|
| 1. The problem and the state of the art | ~1.25 |
| 2. Objectives | ~0.75 |
| 3. Overall strategy — how each objective is reached | ~2.0 |
| 4. Preliminary data / feasibility | ~0.5 (or folded into 3) |
| 5. Risk, ambition and impact | ~0.5 |

Plus one figure, ideally a conceptual schematic on page 1–2, not a data figure.

---

## 1. Problem and state of the art

Open with the question, not the field. The first three sentences should contain: what is
believed, why that belief is inadequate, and what this project will establish instead.

A first paragraph that begins "X is a major cause of morbidity worldwide, affecting N million
people..." wastes the most valuable space in the proposal. Disease burden is context, not
motivation, and every proposal in the pile has it.

End the section with the gap stated as a **specific unanswered question**, not as a literature
deficiency.

## 2. Objectives

Three, displayed prominently — bold, numbered, visually findable when the reader flips back.
Each stated as an outcome ("Establish whether...", "Determine the mechanism by which...", not
"Investigate the role of...").

Immediately after the list, one or two sentences on **why these three together** constitute the
answer to the question in section 1. This is the sentence that converts three good ideas into a
project, and it is the sentence most often missing.

## 3. Overall strategy

For each objective: rationale → approach → what the result would mean, in that order. Roughly
half a page each.

The approach here is at the level of *logic of experiment*, not protocol. Protocol goes in B2.
The test: could a panel member who does not use your techniques still see why the experiment
answers the question? If not, you have written B2 material.

Say explicitly what a negative result would mean. A proposal where only the positive outcome is
interpretable is a proposal that can fail silently.

## 4. Preliminary data

Small, targeted, and doing one job: showing the hardest step works in your hands. Two panels of
one figure is usually enough. This is also where independence gets demonstrated implicitly —
preliminary data generated by you, in your own hands or your own group, is the strongest
available evidence that the project is yours.

## 5. Risk, ambition, impact

Three short paragraphs:

- **Ambition** — restate the "field will have to stop believing X" claim, now that the reader
  has seen how it will be established.
- **Risk** — name the risks and the fallbacks in one or two sentences each. Confidence, not
  defensiveness.
- **Impact** — scientific first. Translational or societal impact second and briefly, and only
  if genuine. ERC does not score societal impact, and overclaiming it signals that the applicant
  has mistaken this for a different funder's scheme.

---

## Craft notes

- **The flip-back test.** A panel member reads at speed, then flips back to find the objectives.
  If they cannot find them in three seconds, the proposal has already lost ground. Use bold,
  white space, and a boxed objective list.
- **One idea per paragraph, and the idea is the first sentence.** A skimmer reading only first
  sentences should get the whole argument.
- **Cut hedging.** "We aim to attempt to begin to characterise" is four hedges. ERC panels read
  hedging as low confidence in the project.
- **References** are within the page limit in the B1 format used in recent calls — confirm
  against the 2027 Information for Applicants and budget the space if so. Cite sparingly and
  compactly; this is not a review.
- **Do not write B1 by compressing B2.** It is the other way round.
