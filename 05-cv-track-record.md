# CV and track record (4 pages)

Read at **Step 1**, alongside B1. It carries half the weight of the assessment, and it is where
proposals with good science quietly fail.

The question the panel is answering is not "has this person published well?" It is:
**"is this person capable of independently leading a group that does something new?"**
Those are different questions, and the CV must answer the second.

## What goes in

- **Personal details, education, positions** — plus career breaks, stated plainly and without
  apology. WP2027 explicitly makes room for career breaks, alternative career paths, and
  non-linear trajectories, and declared breaks change how output-per-year is read.
- **Publications** — not everything. A selected list, with your role made explicit. For each
  key paper, one line on *what it established* and *what your contribution was*.
- **Broader research outputs** — datasets, software, resources, clinical guidelines, patents,
  reagents adopted by others. These count and are explicitly invited.
- **Funding secured**, especially anything you won as PI, however small.
- **Supervision** — students and postdocs supervised, formally or de facto.
- **Recognition** — invited talks (especially international, especially where you were invited
  and not your PI), prizes, editorial or review roles, organising committees.

## Framing independence

This is the crux for StG, and it is largely a framing problem — most applicants have more
evidence of independence than their CV shows.

Evidence panels actually weigh:

1. **Last/corresponding-author papers** — the strongest signal. One is worth a great deal.
2. **Papers without your PhD supervisor or postdoc PI** as a co-author.
3. **Independently won funding**, even modest fellowships, especially where you wrote the science.
4. **Your own students** supervised to output.
5. **Invitations addressed to you** by name, for your own work.
6. **A distinct research direction**, traceable across recent output, that is not the host lab's.

If several of these are thin — common at this career stage, and more common now that the window
opens at year 0 — do not conceal it. State the trajectory: what you did, what you started doing
independently, and what the ERC grant makes possible. A candid, evidenced upward trajectory reads
far better than a CV padded to look established. Panels see hundreds of these and are not fooled
by inflation, but they are persuaded by direction.

## Presentation

- **Do not use bare metrics as the argument.** H-index and journal names as the headline read
  badly with ERC panels, and several signatory institutions treat them as inappropriate. Say what
  the work established.
- **Make roles unambiguous.** Mark co-first authorships, corresponding authorship, and
  contribution in plain words. A panel member should never have to guess what you did.
- **Normalise for time.** If you have a two-year break or a clinical training period, say so,
  and let the reader compute output per active year themselves — they will.
- **Four pages is a lot.** Use the space. A two-page CV in a four-page slot reads as a thin record
  even when the record is not thin.

## Common failures

- A publication list with no interpretation — the panel must reconstruct your contribution.
- Every paper listed, so the important ones are invisible.
- No mention of supervision, so no evidence of ability to lead people.
- Independence asserted ("I have developed an independent line of research") without a single
  piece of supporting evidence.
- Career breaks omitted, so the output gap looks like a productivity gap.
