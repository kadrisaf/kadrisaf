# Idea stress test

Answer these in writing, before drafting B1. Short answers are fine; evasive answers are the
signal. Where an answer is uncomfortable, that is the part the panel will find.

---

## 1. The question

**What is the question?** One sentence, no subordinate clauses, no methods in it.

If the sentence needs a technique in it to make sense ("using single-cell multi-omics, we
will...") the project is currently a method looking for a question. That is the single most
common failure mode in LS panels, and it is fatal at Step 1 — techniques date, and a panel that
sees a technique-led proposal assumes the applicant is following a field rather than leading one.

## 2. Why now, and why not already

- Why has nobody answered this? Name the actual obstacle — conceptual, technical, or a missing
  measurement — not "it has been understudied".
- What has changed that makes it answerable now? Your own prior result, a new tool, a new
  system? If nothing has changed, the panel will ask why this proposal is not five years old.

## 3. The ground-breaking claim

Complete, literally: **"If this project succeeds, the field will have to stop believing X and
start believing Y."**

- If you cannot fill in X, you have an incremental project. Excellent incremental work does not
  win StG; this is worth knowing now, while there is still time to change the framing or the
  project.
- If Y is "we will have a comprehensive map/atlas/characterisation of...", that is a resource,
  not a claim. Resources win ERC grants only when the resource itself overturns something.

## 4. Ambition versus feasibility

The pair of failure modes, and StG proposals die at both ends:

- **Too safe:** the panel can see the results already. "Well-designed, will certainly work,
  outcome largely predictable" is a rejection.
- **Too loose:** ambition unsupported by any evidence you can do it. "Visionary but no evidence
  the PI can deliver" is also a rejection.

The resolution is **preliminary data**. Not data that proves the hypothesis — data that proves
the *hardest step is achievable in your hands*. Which figure does that? If there isn't one, which
one could exist by mid-September?

## 5. Risk, deliberately

Name the riskiest element and say what happens if it fails. Then check: **if the riskiest
objective fails completely, does the project still produce something worth €1.5M?**

- If yes, you have a proper high-risk/high-gain structure.
- If no, restructure the objectives so that it does. This is usually done by making the risky
  objective the third one, resting on two that stand alone.

Do not hide risk. A proposal with no visible risk reads as unambitious, and panels read
unacknowledged risk as risk the PI has not noticed — which is worse than risk itself.

## 6. The objectives

Three (occasionally four). For each:

- One line, starting with a verb, stating what will be *established*, not what will be *done*.
- What does success look like concretely?
- **Does it depend on the previous objective succeeding?**

Serial dependency is the most common structural fault in LS proposals: Obj 2 needs Obj 1's
mouse line, Obj 3 needs Obj 2's screen. A panel reads that as one project with three chances
to fail. Restructure so objectives are parallel and mutually reinforcing.

## 7. Is it yours?

The panel will silently ask whether this is your PhD supervisor's or postdoc PI's project.

- Where does this depart from your previous lab's programme?
- Would your former PI regard this as competing with them? (If plainly yes, say so and address
  it. If plainly no, ask yourself whether you have separated at all.)
- Do you have output — papers, ideally with you as corresponding or last author, or a first-author
  paper whose direction was demonstrably yours — that marks the departure?

Independence is assessed as much from the science as from the CV. A brilliant proposal that is
visibly a continuation of the host lab's programme scores badly on the PI criterion.

## 8. The 90-second version

Say the whole thing out loud in 90 seconds to someone in your panel but not your subfield. What
did they not follow, and what did they say back to you? The sentence they repeat back is the one
that belongs at the top of B1.

---

## Panel choice

- Which panel, and who on it (from published membership of recent calls) could act as your
  rapporteur? At Step 1 your fate largely rests on the two or three members assigned to read you.
- Is there a panel where you are the *closest fit anyone has*, versus one where you are one of
  many? Prefer the former, even if the fit is imperfect on paper.
- Keywords in Part A should steer assignment towards those people. They are not decoration.
